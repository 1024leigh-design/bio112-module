Step 1

Find & copy the gene

First you need a copy of the gene you want. For genes from eukaryotes like us, scientists start with the mRNA and copy it back into DNA.

From gene to cDNA

Click through the four stages. Purple tags happen inside the cell; blue tags happen in the lab.

Reverse transcriptase: the UNO reverse card

Normal transcription goes DNA → RNA. Reverse transcriptase runs it backward, building DNA from an RNA template. It needs an RNA template, a primer, and dNTPs.

It's found in retroviruses like HIV. It was first discovered in 1970 in retroviruses that cause tumors in animals, years before HIV was identified.

Why bother with cDNA?

Mature mRNA has already had its introns spliced out. Bacteria can't remove introns, so we hand them cDNA ("complementary DNA"): an intron-free DNA version of the gene they can read straight through.

Reverse transcriptase is also the "RT" in RT-PCR, like the COVID test in Step 4.

Steps 2 & 3

Build the vector, cut it, insert the gene

A vector is a DNA molecule that carries foreign DNA into a cell, usually a plasmid (sometimes a virus). Think of it as a USB drive for DNA.

What does our vector need?

1. The gene of interest

The DNA you actually want carried into the cell.

2. A self-replicating backbone

A plasmid with an origin of replication, so the host copies it on its own.

3. A way to tell it worked

A color change or an antibiotic resistance gene. More in Step 6.

Plasmids are small, circular DNA molecules that copy themselves independently of the chromosome. Click each part of the map.

Step 2: Cut it with EcoRI

Restriction enzymes are molecular scissors. Each one recognizes a short, specific, usually palindromic sequence and cuts the DNA backbone there. EcoRI recognizes GAATTC. Plasmids come preloaded with these restriction sites.

Your turn: find GAATTC on the top strand, then click the spot between two bases where EcoRI cuts.

Reading guide

Cutting DNA with a restriction enzyme: endergonic or exergonic? Catabolic or anabolic? What happens to activation energy?

Cutting DNA breaks bonds, so it's catabolic, and it releases energy, so it's exergonic. The enzyme lowers the activation energy, which speeds up a reaction that was already favorable. Classic enzyme behavior: bind the substrate at the active site, form an enzyme-substrate complex, use induced fit to stabilize the transition state, release the products, and the enzyme is free to go again.

Recombinant DNA

Recombinant means "recombined": one DNA molecule built from DNA from two different sources. This recombinant plasmid is what gets amplified next and then carried into a host cell.

Step 4

Amplify with PCR

Transformations are done on a LOT of cells, so we need a LOT of plasmid. PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies the recombinant plasmid millions of times before it goes into bacteria. After that, the bacteria keep copying it every time they divide.

PCR is DNA replication, in a tube

In your cells, replication needs helicase, primase, and DNA polymerase. PCR swaps in heat and lab-made ingredients for each job.

From the slides

Why does DNA split into two strands when heated, instead of falling apart (depolymerizing)?

The two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs, which are weak and break at about 94–96°C. Each strand's own backbone is held together by covalent phosphodiester bonds, which are much stronger and survive the heat. So the strands separate, but each strand stays intact.

Semi-conservative, just like in cells

Meselson and Stahl (1958) showed each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand and gains one new strand. Watch the PCR animation: every copy is one purple (old) strand plus one blue (new) strand.

Step 5

Transform the plasmid into cells

Transformation is a cell taking up DNA from its surroundings. Cells that are able to do this are called competent cells. Once they take in the plasmid, they are recombinant.

The heat-shock method

Click through each stage and watch the thermometer, the membrane, and the plasmids.

Other ways to get DNA into a cell

Electroporation

A brief electric pulse opens temporary pores in the membrane so DNA can get in. It works on bacteria directly, cell walls and all. Plant cells, with their thick walls, are often turned into protoplasts first.

Protoplasts & fusion

Enzymes strip off the cell wall, leaving a protoplast (just a fragile membrane). Polyethylene glycol fuses two protoplasts, their chromosomes recombine, and a new wall grows back. Most common in plants and algae.

Microinjection

A glass micropipette punctures the membrane and injects DNA directly. Used on larger cells, like animal eggs or embryos. It's also how CRISPR mixes get into animals.

Model organism: C. elegansA transparent roundworm, about 1 mm long, that eats bacteria. It was the first multicellular organism to have its whole genome sequenced, and all 302 neurons of its nervous system are mapped. It's see-through with a short life cycle, so it's easy to microinject DNA into its gonad and watch the changes in its offspring.

Step 6

Selection: did it work?

Not every cell takes up the plasmid, and not every plasmid has the gene inserted. Plasmid vectors carry marker genes so you can tell. Most protocols use dual selection, kind of like two-factor authentication.

Primary selection: antibiotic

The host bacteria are naturally sensitive to the antibiotic. The plasmid carries a resistance gene: ampicillin is the most common, and tetracycline and kanamycin work the same way. Only transformed cells grow on the selective medium.

Secondary selection: is the gene in there?

Growing on antibiotic only proves a cell has a plasmid. Secondary selection checks for the insert:

Color change (GFP, X-gal) · DNA probes and chips (labeled DNA that binds complementary sequences; chips hold arrays of probes) · Gene product analysis (directly detects the protein; examples include DNA sequencing, Western blots, northern blots, and ELISA).

Step 7a

Harvest copies of the gene

Once you have the right clone, the bacteria do the copying for you. Every time a cell divides, it copies its plasmid too, so the culture becomes a factory of gene copies.

What the copies are used for

Into other organisms

A gene encoding a protein for pest resistance is inserted into plant cells (like Bt corn, in Applications).

Cleanup crews

A gene encoding an enzyme that breaks down toxic waste is inserted into bacterial cells to help clean it up.

Loops back to Step 1Harvested gene copies also feed cDNA libraries and new rounds of cloning.

If our column had cations (positive charges) as probes, what particles would end up in the final purification?

Opposite charges attract, so the positive column traps negatively charged (anionic) particles. Neutral and positive particles flow through. Raising the salt releases the negative particles, so your final purification holds the negatively charged ones. Try the ion exchange column above to see it.

Lab
Lab example · dual selection

X-gal blue-white selection

X-gal selection is built on the lac operon. It turns "did my gene actually insert?" into something you can see: a blue colony or a white one.

The vector

1. Ampicillin resistance gene: primary selection. Only transformed cells survive.

2. lacZ gene, broken by an MCS: lacZ codes for β-galactosidase. The multiple cloning site (MCS), a cluster of upwards of 20 restriction sites, sits inside lacZ. Inserting your gene there breaks lacZ, so it's non-functional.

What's in the media

Ampicillin: selects transformed cells.

IPTG: mimics lactose and induces lacZ expression.

X-gal: a chromogenic substrate for β-galactosidase. When it's cleaved, it makes an insoluble blue molecule, turning the colony blue.

The lac operon: inducible negative control

Toggle IPTG to see what happens at the operator.

Lab example · case study

pGLO transformation lab

The pGLO lab runs the same pipeline you just walked through, with a striking payoff: bacteria that glow.

The pGLO plasmid

Ampicillin resistance gene: primary selection, same as always.

GFP gene: green fluorescent protein, originally from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria. GFP is controlled by the pBAD promoter, which only switches on when the sugar arabinose is present.

Same logic as X-gal, different sugarpBAD is switched on by arabinose the way the lac operon is switched on by lactose (or IPTG). A sugar in the environment turns a promoter on.

Gene editing in eukaryotes

CRISPR & gene editing

Gene editing changes an organism's DNA at specific locations to get a desired trait. Modern tools use guided enzymes to find their target. The most famous one, CRISPR-Cas9, started out as an immune system in bacteria.

Where CRISPR comes from: bacteria with a memory

Bacteria get attacked by viruses called bacteriophages. CRISPR lets a bacterium remember a virus it has seen before and destroy it faster the next time. Click through the five stages.

What the name meansClustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats. The repeats are short palindromic sequences (gray). In between them, at regular intervals, sit the spacers (colors): stored pieces of viral DNA.

Just like your immune system's memory

Both are adaptive immunity: protection learned from a past infection.

From yogurt bacteria to a Nobel Prize

Scientists spotted CRISPR in bacteria decades ago. Then two researchers figured out how to scoop it up and reprogram it. Click a year.

CRISPR-Cas9 in the lab

Scientists isolate Cas9 and give it a new target. A lab-made guide RNA (gRNA) replaces the bacterium's CRISPR RNA, so Cas9 cuts wherever the gRNA sends it.

Step

Not a restriction enzyme

EcoRI always cuts the same sequence (GAATTC). Cas9 has no fixed sequence: it cuts wherever its guide RNA base-pairs. Swap the guide and you change the target.

The master mix

A tube of Cas9/gRNA, salts, and a repair template is injected into animals (microinjection!) or applied directly to cells. The cell's own DNA repair puts in the new DNA.
